# Three readings for the agentic RL thread: Steel, ScheduleGen, MuJoCo 4

2026-11-14. Companion to `AGENTIC_RL_FORMULA_20261113.md` (the formula) and `AGENTIC_RL_IDEAS_20261114.md` (ten ideas). Nothing ran on the rig or in the sim for this page. The only new numbers of our own are stage times that we read from three existing real run logs.

## Short answer

1. **ScheduleGen is the most useful reading.** It treats a multi-arm task as skills with precedence rules and solves for the timing. It reports 14.98 % fewer steps from retiming alone (688 of 700 trajectories). Our graph has the same structure and is fully serial across the two arms. Our logs show about 17 s of pure serial arm moves (capture and home) in a run of 110 to 114 s.
2. **Steel is a speed result, not an accuracy result.** Its write-up claims throughput only ("Simulation throughput, not end-to-end training speed"). It reports no accuracy number and no sim-to-real number. We should take its agent procedures for tuning and parity checks. We should not change the solver.
3. **MuJoCo 4 does not change our contact node today.** The headline contact feature (speculative contacts) is for fast or thin objects and is CPU only. Three smaller items matter: `multiccd` by default with margins, a rewritten box-box collider, and a `mujoco.sysid` toolbox. Our stack stays on 3.12 until MuJoCo Warp support is verified.
4. **None of the three readings shows a sub-millimetre real insertion.** Conclusion 2 of the formula stays.
5. **The formula's line 6 should grow** from "optimise the graph's numbers" to "optimise the graph's numbers and its schedule". The proposal is in section A.

## Reading 1: Steel (Skild AI)

**What it is.** A GPU rigid-body solver for manipulation simulation. Skild AI built it with NVIDIA. The solver code is in Newton (`newton/_src/solvers/steel`). The repository `skild-ai/steel` holds examples, documents and agent procedures. Open-source launch: 9 November 2026. Licence: Apache-2.0. No paper is linked.

**Method (from the documents).**

- Articulations use joint coordinates (Featherstone dynamics, a Cholesky factor of the mass matrix).
- Contacts, limits and drives are rows of a velocity-level projected Gauss-Seidel solve. The default is 12 iterations. Positions follow by symplectic Euler.
- The sweep is matrix-free. It reuses each articulation's factor and builds no dense constraint matrix.
- Friction "anchors": contacts of one body pair with similar normals form a region. At most two anchors carry the friction of a region. Each anchor tracks its tangential displacement, so "static friction holds position, not just zero velocity". With per-point friction the document says "a held object creeps".
- A run-to-run determinism mode gives bitwise identical repeats on the same GPU model, driver and Warp build.

**Hardware and data.** Simulation only. Benchmarks ran on one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU. No robot and no real data.

**Results that the source reports.**

| Claim | Number | Conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Throughput against PhysX | 3.8 times (geometric mean, 20 single tasks); faster on 19 of 20 | Physics step only; no observations, rewards, resets or cameras |
| Throughput against MuJoCo Warp | about 1.3 times; MuJoCo Warp is 2.9 times PhysX | MuJoCo Warp 3.17 |
| Kuka-Allegro lift (23 joints) | 6.7 times PhysX, about 1.8 times MuJoCo Warp | Same |
| Mixed batches of 20 task types | 13.0 times PhysX at peak; 3.61 M against 300 K env-steps/s at 61,440 envs | One 5 ms step per env step; each engine at its best batch size |
| GPU memory | "about 62% less" at 61,440 envs (about 11 against 29 GiB) | Same |

The write-up gives no accuracy metric, no sim-to-real result and no n for a robot task. Its demos (cable, cloth, hydroelastic grasp) are qualitative.

**One example is close to our task.** `contacts_rj45_plug`: an RJ45 plug goes into a socket with a hinged latch, with SDF mesh contact. Its preset file says Steel alone is "unsupported" for this scene, because the cable is a rod. It runs with the VBD solver, or with Steel and VBD coupled. The example sets friction to 0 (`mu=0.0`), a contact stiffness of 1e5 and a contact gap of 2 mm. Its check is that the latch deflects and holds. It is not a measured insertion force.

**How it relates to the formula.**

- Line 4 (train the contact policy in sim on a real-like plant): Steel changes how fast the sim runs, not how real the plant is.
- Step 2 and step 5 of the loop (gate in sim; find the node): the repository's `agents/` folder is a worked example of what we want. It has three layers: tools (code), steps (one procedure with a pass rule) and workflows (a goal with an ordered list of steps). Every file opens with "Does / Use in / Passes when".

**What we should take.**

1. *The parity check order.* The step `check-parity` compares two traces by name and finds "the first step and the first quantity that leaves tolerance; that is where to look, not the final outcome". It audits causes in a fixed order: the stack (reset timing, quaternion conventions, stale history), then options, actuation, forces, materials, contacts, capacity. This is our cause-finding step with an order that we do not have in writing.
2. *One factor at a time.* The step `randomize-one-factor` turns on one randomisation factor, compares per-sample values, and runs the full randomisation only after each factor passes alone. Our contact-model sensitivity test (formula, ranked item 1) should use this design.
3. *The validation list of the tuning guide.* Require zero dropped constraint rows. "Finite state alone isn't success." Test at the world count that you run, with at least 64 worlds for grasp checks. Compare against another solver before you blame the solver. Our environment does not log the CCD overflow bit (project notes), so we fail the first rule today.
4. *The convergence test.* Halve the substeps at the same iterations. Then halve the substeps at twice the iterations. If only the second passes, the scene is limited by solver convergence, not by the time step. We have not run this test on the insertion contact.
5. *A stop rule for tuning.* "If the scene still fails at 32 substeps and 48 iterations, stop changing settings: it is a scene or solver problem." A written stop rule belongs in our fix-class rule.
6. *A caution about repeats.* "Fixed seeds do not make GPU rollouts bitwise identical." "A one-world rerun is a different experiment." This agrees with our A-S8 finding.

**What does not transfer.**

- A change of solver. Steel solves hard contact at velocity level with a Baumgarte correction. Its own guide says thin objects can sink into supports, and that residual penetration stays above position-based solvers for heavy-on-light cases. Our clearance is under 0.1 mm. No evidence says Steel is more accurate than MuJoCo Warp at that scale.
- The speed gain. About 1.3 times MuJoCo Warp does not change what we can train. Our limit is fidelity, not steps per second.
- The PhysX porting workflow. We do not use PhysX.

## Reading 2: ScheduleGen (NVIDIA, UT Austin)

**What it is.** A method that generates multi-arm demonstrations in simulation for imitation learning. Paper dated 2026-10-28 (PDF on the project page). Authors: Jawale, Pleines, Pouya, Zhu, Mandlekar, Garrett. No arXiv number and no code link are on the page.

**Method.** Four steps.

1. *Adapt skills.* A skill is one arm's contact segment, stored relative to an object frame. The method moves it to the new object pose (as MimicGen does).
2. *Plan motion.* Each arm gets a joint-space path that connects its skills. Collisions between moving arms are not checked yet.
3. *Schedule arms.* A mixed-integer linear program sets the time of every waypoint. The objective is the makespan (the latest arm end time), plus an optional second cost. The constraints are: path order, task precedence between skills, synchronisation, a minimum time step, and a collision rule. The collision rule says: if waypoint i of arm r collides with waypoint j of arm r', one arm must finish before the other starts, plus a safety margin. The minimum time step "prevents scheduling from increasing waypoint-to-waypoint speed". GPU sphere checks (cuRobo) find the colliding pairs. The solver is not named.
4. *Validate.* Each schedule is replayed in parallel simulation. Only successful runs are kept.

Skills can come from four sources: segmented teleoperation, scripted waypoints, grasp waypoints, or "agentically authoring skills".

**Hardware.** Simulation: bimanual YAM, bimanual and four-arm DROID, a GR1T2 humanoid with a fixed waist (Isaac Lab); four robosuite variants (MuJoCo); seven RoboDojo tasks on an X5 bimanual setup. Real: a bimanual YAM setup, three RGB views, joint-position targets. This is the same arm type as ours.

**Data needed.** Five annotated source demonstrations per task. 1,000 generated demonstrations per variant (500 per RoboDojo task). The four-arm task uses motion-planned segments in place of teleoperation. The sim-to-real policies "use no real demonstrations"; they need a calibrated robot, camera, workspace and object geometry.

**Results that the source reports.**

| Result | Number | n |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simulation, all variants | 79.6 % mean data-generation success, 65.5 % mean policy success | 28 variants |
| Cross-arm layouts against DexMimicGen-Tuned (policy success) | Bowl 80 against 42 %; dishware 76 against 58 %; cube 86 against 74 %; cube with bin 70 against 0 % | 50 episodes per policy |
| RoboDojo "Insert Tubes" (simulation only) | 94.2 % generation, 45 % policy (nominal), 20 % (perturbed) | 20 episodes per condition |
| Concurrency in generated data | Both arms move in 31.5 % of steps, against 15.5 % in the released demonstrations; 7.5 % shorter on average | 3,500 trajectories |
| Retiming alone, paths and task order fixed | 688 of 700 feasible; 14.98 % fewer steps in total; 16.44 % mean per trajectory; per task 9.92 to 33.71 % | 700 trajectories; "task completion is not checked through physics replay" |
| Sim-to-real on the real YAM, no real demonstrations | Bowl 75 and 80 %, dishware 80 and 75 % | 20 episodes per policy |
| Real-to-sim-to-real | Cube stacking 65 %; trash 75, 70 and 50 % | 20 episodes per policy |

The paper has no limitations section. It does not report a tolerance for any task. The real tasks are stacking and placing.

**How it relates to the formula.**

- Their "skill plan" (skills plus precedence constraints) is our graph (stages plus gates). The precedence constraints are the part that our graph does not write down. Our chain is a fixed serial order in code.
- Their scheduling step is a new kind of graph optimisation. The formula's section "Optimising the graph itself" lists four things to optimise: stage numbers, hand-over conditions, discrete choices, retry choice. The schedule is a fifth.
- Their retiming result is the nearest published number to our case: fixed paths, fixed task order, time only. It gave 15 to 16 %.
- Their last step (replay every schedule in simulation and keep the successes) is our dry-run gate.

**What we should take.**

1. *Write the dependencies.* For each stage: which output of which earlier stage it needs, and which arm it moves. Everything else may overlap. Section A does this for our graph.
2. *Retime, do not speed up.* The minimum time step keeps every arm at its own speed. This fits our rule that a move speed is a limit that only the operator changes.
3. *The collision rule as a wait.* When two paths conflict, one arm waits. The schedule never changes a path. This is a small, safe change to a serial chain.
4. *A second cost for smooth motion.* With the same makespan, their "continuity" cost removed internal pauses (longest pause 0.92 s to 0 s; summed active time 17.00 to 15.40 s; one task). Stops and restarts are where our joints stick and slip, so fewer pauses can help the approach as well.
5. *The concurrency fraction as a metric.* The share of ticks in which both arms move. Ours is close to 0 % outside the hold.

**What does not transfer.**

- The purpose. ScheduleGen makes training data for a visuomotor policy. We run the schedule itself on the rig.
- The data need. Five source demonstrations per task; we have zero real demonstrations and want to keep that. (The scripted and agent-authored skill sources would fit us; the paper reports no result for the agent-authored source.)
- The precision. No real task is precise. "Insert Tubes" reaches 45 % in simulation (20 episodes).
- The MILP. Our graph has two arms and about ten stages. A hand-written precedence table and a clearance check are enough. A solver is worth it only when the graph has branches or more arms.
- The collision check alone. Spheres check geometry. They do not check a press force, a camera view or a net's training distribution. Our overlaps have such non-geometric dependencies (section A).

### Safety rules for a schedule

1. Bases clamped. No change.
2. One operator go per motion command. A two-arm move is one command with one go, as the capture move is today. The operator must approve this reading of the rule before any rig run.
3. No safety gate changes. No speed limit changes: `move_max_vel_rad_s` stays 0.4. The schedule changes start times only (ScheduleGen's minimum time step rule).
4. No two arms move in a shared workspace without a clearance check. The check is on the swept paths, with a margin, and it runs in the dry run and again from the measured start poses before the go.
5. If one arm stops (guard, e-stop, gate), both arms hold.
6. A stage that can fail (the pick) does not start an irreversible step of the other arm (the press).

### Sim-only experiments

**E-A1. Offline retiming on logged paths (1 day, CPU).** Take `moves_left.npz` and `moves_right.npz` of all real runs with logs. For the capture and the home, shift the second arm's path in time from fully serial to fully parallel. At every tick compute the minimum distance between the two arms (sphere models of the links and grippers, and the PC). Output: the makespan and the minimum clearance per offset.
- Pass: full overlap keeps a clearance of 50 mm or more on every run, or a fixed lead does.
- Falsified if: no offset under 4 s keeps the clearance, or the saving is under 10 s for P1 + P2.

**E-A2. Dry-run A/B with paired seeds (1 to 2 days).** Add the parallel moves behind a config key that is off by default (a new config file, no edit in place). Run `deploy.run_bimanual --dry-run` with the serial and the parallel config on the same seeds (7041 to 7045 and 10 more). Compare: outcome, stage times, minimum clearance, the guards that fire, the hand-over error.
- Pass: no new stop; the same outcome on every seed where the serial run is stable; a saving of 15 s or more.
- Falsified if: a new guard stop appears, or the hand-over lateral error grows by more than 0.2 mm (median).
- Limit: the dry run does not contain joint stick-slip or the real camera. P3 needs a frame test as well: render the right wrist view with the left arm at the stand-off and run the servo-pick nets on it.

**E-A3. Press-time budget (half a day, offline).** From the logs: the time from the latch to the release on every run, with and without the alignment's press-off interval. This gives the room for the larger form of P3.
- Falsified if: the press time plus the remaining pick time passes 50 s on any logged run. Then only the 2.0 s swing is allowed.

### Formula line 6, proposed text

> 6. *Optimise the graph's numbers and its schedule.* Use an optimiser on replayed real logs for the numbers. For the schedule, write what each stage needs from which earlier stage and which arm it moves. Let stages with no dependency overlap, after a clearance check on the swept paths. Change start times only: never a speed, a path or a safety gate.

Three additions to the section "Optimising the graph itself":

- A fifth row in the table of what can be optimised: *Stage start times.* Examples: two-arm capture and home moves, the left arm's swing during the pick. Method: a precedence table and a clearance check; a MILP only if the graph gets branches or more arms. Where it runs: offline on logged joint paths, then paired dry runs, then 3 rig runs.
- A fifth replay: *path replay*. Logged joint paths of both arms are shifted in time and checked for clearance.
- A new limit: *the dependency must include what the cameras and nets need*, not only geometry. A ZED net with an arm-pose gate is a dependency on the other arm's pose.

## B. Accurate physics

### What the readings say

- **TI-0.1** (reviewed in the formula): a frozen policy went from 84.5 % to 2.6 % when the pad stiffness, travel and friction were revised (2,048 episodes per condition, sim). The report also says that high precision needs "finer geometry and contact fidelity than our current reconstructions provide". So accurate physics here means: the result must not rest on one contact model.
- **Steel**: no accuracy claim. Its documents give methods to find out if a result rests on solver settings: zero dropped rows, the convergence test, the parity check, one factor at a time, at least 64 worlds for grasp checks. Its friction document names one effect that we should look for: with per-point friction "a held object creeps", and a box near its friction limit slides.
- **MuJoCo 4**: contact generation changed (`multiccd` default, new box-box collider, stability fixes that "can slightly change contacts"). Speculative contacts are not for our case. A simulator can state an error against a known answer (0.5 % for the rod). A system identification toolbox exists.

None of the three gives a contact stiffness, a friction value or an insertion force for a USB-C class connector. None validates a tight insertion against a real measurement.

### What applies to our MuJoCo Warp insertion sim

1. We do not know if K4's sim result is converged in the solver settings. We have never run the convergence test on the insertion contact.
2. We do not log constraint or CCD overflow per world. A dropped contact is silent. Steel's rule (zero dropped rows) is a gate that we lack.
3. Our four open sim problems are of two kinds. Two are missing physics: arm and grasp compliance (rig 0.25 to 0.29 mm/N, sim about 10 times stiffer) and the unknown real insertion force. Two may be numerics: contact fidelity in the grasp and the port, and the release effect (the plug is seated at the end of the insertion and not seated after the jaws open, in 2 of 4 dry runs with K7). The readings give tests for the numerics kind. Only a real measurement fixes the first kind.
4. MuJoCo contacts are soft constraints. A held part can move slowly under load, and a contact can store a small penetration that is released when the load ends. This statement is from our general knowledge of MuJoCo, not from the three readings. It is a candidate cause of the release effect, and the Steel note about creep points the same way.

### Experiments for the contact node

**E-B1. Solver convergence and overflow test on frozen K4 and K7 (1 day, GPU).** Cells: time step 1 ms and 0.5 ms; solver iterations at today's value and twice; line-search iterations at today's value and twice. n = 1,024 per cell at the real spot, the same starts. Also log per world: active contacts against `nconmax`, constraints against `njmax`, the CCD overflow bit. Measures: seat rate, seat time, the peak penetration between plug and port, the contact force along the port axis, the plug's motion in the jaws.
- Pass: the seat rate moves by less than 2 points between cells; the peak penetration is under half the clearance (0.05 mm); zero overflow.
- Falsified (the result rests on solver settings) if: a cell changes the seat rate by 2 points or more, or any world has an overflow in a failed run.

**E-B2. Contact-model sensitivity, one factor at a time (1 day, GPU).** This is ranked item 1 of the formula with Steel's design. Factors, each alone, then all: pad model (box, 4 spheres, soft pad), pad friction range, port friction range, contact `solref` and `solimp` of the plug-port pair, `condim`, `impratio`. n = 1,024 per cell, frozen K4 and K7.
- Pass: no single factor moves the seat rate by more than 5 points inside its plausible range.
- Falsified (the 12 of 12 can rest on a sim-only effect) if: one factor does. Then that factor gets a real measurement before the placements are widened.

**E-B3. The release effect (1 to 2 days, GPU and CPU).** Save 1,024 sim states at the end of the insertion, seated. Open the jaws under each variant: today's settings; half the time step; stiffer contact `solref`; higher `impratio`; elliptic against pyramidal cones; the left press kept on against released; a slow unload. Measure the plug's motion along the port axis in the 0.5 s after the jaws open, and the stored penetration before. Run 64 of the states in CPU MuJoCo as a parity check.
- Pass: one variant removes the effect (not seated after release under 1 %) and names the cause.
- Falsified (the cause is not contact numerics) if: no variant changes the rate by more than a factor of 2. Then look at the geometry of the sim port and at K4's last actions.
- Why it matters: the rig also shows a 2 mm band after the release. If the sim effect is numerics, it is not evidence about the rig. If it is physical (a stored elastic load), it may be the same effect.

**E-B4. Compliance as a measured element, with a known answer (2 to 3 days; needs the logged pushes, no new rig run).** Add one elastic element for the arm and grasp (a stiff spring joint near the wrist or a compliant grasp), fitted to the logged force pushes (10 N and 14 N) and position pushes. State the error against the measured line, as MuJoCo 4 does for its rod: the sim spring-back at 10 N and 14 N against 1.93 and 3.37 mm. Read `mujoco.sysid` first (half a day) and use it if it fits the data.
- Pass: the sim reproduces both spring-backs within 0.3 mm and holds on 3 held-out pushes.
- Falsified (one linear element is not enough) if: the held-out error is over 0.5 mm. Then the compliance is not linear, or it is in the grasp and not in the arm.
- This is ranked item 3 of the formula and idea 5 of the ideas page. The new part is the stated error and the tool check.

**Not proposed.** A move to MuJoCo 4, Newton or Steel. One optional check stays cheap: CPU MuJoCo 4.0 in a separate environment (not the shared one) on the box-pad contact case, about half a day. It tells us if the sphere pads are still needed after a later upgrade.
